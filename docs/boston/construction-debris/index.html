The short version

Boston's trash crews won't take renovation waste. The City says it does not collect construction debris, automotive parts, plumbing fixtures, fences, water heaters or fire debris, and tells residents to find a local private hauler boston.gov[1].

On top of that, Massachusetts bans several common building materials from the trash entirely, so a hauler has to send them to recycling instead of a landfill mass.gov[2].

Your realistic options are a junk-removal crew that loads it for you, or a rented dumpster (which needs a City permit if it sits on the street).

What counts, and what's banned

Boston lists these as items a private hauler must collect boston.gov[1]:

  • Asphalt pavement, brick and concrete
  • Treated and untreated wood and wood waste
  • Clean gypsum wallboard (drywall)
  • Ferrous and non-ferrous metals

These line up with MassDEP's statewide disposal bans: asphalt pavement, brick and concrete, clean gypsum wallboard, and metals are banned from disposal or transfer for disposal, and wood is banned from landfills mass.gov[2]. MassDEP says both the people who generate waste and the haulers who transport it are responsible for following the bans mass.gov[2].

The City also won't take plumbing fixtures, water heaters, fences, automotive parts or fire debris boston.gov[1]. Dishwashers, water heaters and plumbing fixtures are on the City's list of things to donate or give to a private hauler boston.gov[7].

Why you can't just bag it

Boston requires all trash to fit inside a lidded black or gray barrel, and an overflowing barrel can earn a code enforcement ticket boston.gov[1]. Construction debris left at the curb won't be collected because the City doesn't collect it at all boston.gov[1].

Zero Waste Days, the City's drop-off events, also don't accept construction debris or asbestos boston.gov[3]. They're still useful for the leftover oil-based paint, solvents and chemicals from a project; see Boston's hazardous waste drop-off guide.

Want it hauled away?

The City sends construction debris to private haulers. A junk-removal crew can load drywall, wood, tile, fixtures and the rest from inside your home and haul it the same day, with no container on the street. Confirm they serve your ZIP, ask how they handle Massachusetts waste-ban materials, and check the price before you book.

Renting a dumpster: the permit rules

On the street or sidewalk

A dumpster on a public way needs a Street Occupancy Permit from Boston Public Works boston.gov[6] boston.gov[4].

  • Apply at least 7 business days before you need the space boston.gov[4].
  • Be ready to say how many dumpsters, what size, and whether you'll use a chute boston.gov[4].
  • You must show proof that you're bonded boston.gov[4]. For storage containers on the street, you need a letter of authorization from the company that owns them, and that company must hold a bond with the City boston.gov[4].

What it costs

A $50 base fee plus 10 cents per square foot per day for the first 500 square feet (5 cents after that). Signs are $4 each, and metered spaces are $20 per meter per day boston.gov[5]. From the City's table boston.gov[5]:

  • One space (160 sq ft) for 1 day: $66
  • One space for 7 days: $162
  • Two spaces for 7 days: $274
  • One space for 30 days: $530

Questions go to the Public Works Permitting Division at 617-635-4911 boston.gov[5].

On private property

Inspectional Services says any residential or commercial property using a dumpster needs a Site Cleanliness License ($50), but contractors and developers don't need one for construction dumpsters boston.gov[6]. If you're a homeowner renting your own, ask ISD or your dumpster company which applies.

Frequently asked questions

Will Boston pick up construction debris with the trash?
No. The City says it doesn't collect construction debris, automotive parts, plumbing fixtures, fences, water heaters or fire debris, and asks residents to find a local private hauler. boston.gov[1]
Can I throw drywall or wood in the trash in Massachusetts?
Clean gypsum wallboard is banned from disposal in Massachusetts, and treated and untreated wood is banned from landfills. Boston lists both, along with asphalt, brick, concrete and metals, as materials a private hauler must collect. mass.gov[2] boston.gov[1]
Can I take construction debris to a Boston Zero Waste Day?
No. Zero Waste Days don't accept construction debris, asbestos or commercial waste. They do take things like oil-based paint, chemicals and tires. boston.gov[3]
Do I need a permit for a construction dumpster in Boston?
If it sits on a street or sidewalk, yes: a Street Occupancy Permit from Public Works, applied for at least seven business days ahead, with proof of a bond. A dumpster kept on private property may need a $50 Site Cleanliness License from Inspectional Services, though contractors and developers don't need one for construction dumpsters. boston.gov[4] boston.gov[6]
How much does a dumpster street permit cost in Boston?
A $50 base fee plus 10 cents per square foot per day for the first 500 square feet. The City's table puts one parking space at $66 for one day and $162 for seven days. Metered spaces add $20 per meter per day and signs are $4 each. boston.gov[5]
What about an old toilet, sink or water heater?
The City doesn't collect water heaters or plumbing fixtures, and it lists dishwashers, water heaters and plumbing fixtures as items to donate or give to a private hauler. boston.gov[1] boston.gov[7]
